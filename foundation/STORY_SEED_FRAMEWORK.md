## 1. What a Story Seed Is

A Story Seed is a single, sensory or experiential prompt offered by the Orchestrator when a conversation has reached a natural resting point. It is not a survey item. It is not a recovery technique for a stalled session. It is an invitation — one at a time, offered once, easy to decline.

The purpose is to open memory through experience rather than chronology. Research in autobiographical memory consistently shows that sensory cues (smell, taste, sound, texture) activate richer and more detailed recall than factual prompts ("what year did you move?"). Seeds are written to enter the experiential rather than the administrative.

Seeds are never offered:
- Mid-story, while someone is actively sharing
- As a list ("I could ask you about food, or music, or...")
- In a way that implies the person is behind, lacking, or expected to answer
- Twice in the same session for the same topic cluster

A seed is offered once. If declined or not engaged with, the Orchestrator moves to a different thread or waits for the person to lead.

## 2. Seed Categories and Example Prompts

### 2.1 Smells and Senses

Sensory memory is among the most persistent and emotionally rich. Smell and sound cues in particular retrieve memories that purely factual prompts cannot reach. These seeds are designed to open the door through the body rather than the mind.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Is there a smell from your childhood that you'd recognise anywhere?"
2. "What did your grandmother's house smell like when you came in from outside?"
3. "Was there a particular sound — something you heard regularly growing up — that you still think about?"
4. "What did winter feel like where you grew up? Not the weather — the feeling of it."
5. "Was there a taste that meant something to you — a specific food or season that brings something back?"

---

### 2.2 Music

Music is temporally anchored in a way few other memories are. A song fixes a moment. These seeds are not about musical taste — they are about occasion, memory, and meaning.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Was there a song that played at an important moment in your life — something you still remember hearing?"
2. "Was there music that was always around when you were growing up? Something your family played?"
3. "Did anyone in your family sing, or play an instrument?"
4. "Is there a singer or a band that takes you somewhere specific when you hear them now?"

---

### 2.3 Recipes and Food

Food carries cultural identity, family structure, and tradition in a single dish. A recipe question is rarely just about food — it is about who made it, under what circumstances, and what it meant. These seeds are designed to arrive there from the food itself.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Was there a dish that someone in your family made that you've never tasted anywhere else?"
2. "What did a special occasion meal look like in your household — what was on the table?"
3. "Did anyone teach you to cook something? Who, and what was the dish?"
4. "Was there a food you loved as a child that you don't see anymore?"
5. "Is there a recipe you've tried to pass on, or that you wish someone had written down before it was lost?"

---

### 2.4 Holidays and Traditions

Recurring rituals compress years of family life into a repeating pattern. These seeds are about the pattern — not the single event. What was consistent? What was always the same?

**Example prompts:**

1. "What did a typical holiday look like in your house growing up — who was there, what happened?"
2. "Was there something your family did every year that other families didn't do?"
3. "Who was in charge of a particular holiday — the person who organized it, made the food, set the tone?"
4. "Was there a holiday tradition that didn't survive — something you did as a child that stopped?"
5. "What did New Year's Eve look like in your family?"

---

### 2.5 Pets

Animals are present in families in a way that often goes unrecorded — they do not appear in official documents but they shape daily life and carry their own emotional weight. These seeds treat animals as part of the story, not as a quirky aside.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Did your family have any animals when you were growing up?"
2. "Was there a particular animal — a pet or a working animal — that was important to someone in your family?"
3. "Did a pet play a role in a specific memory — something you've thought about since?"
4. "What happened to the animals in your family during a move or a difficult time?"

---

### 2.6 Places

Place is not geography — it is identity. Where someone is from, where they were displaced from, where they chose to go, and where they never returned to are all different kinds of place memory. These seeds avoid treating place as simply location.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Is there a place you've never really left — somewhere you still think of as home even if you haven't been there in years?"
2. "What did the street or neighbourhood you grew up on look like? Who else was there?"
3. "Was there a place your family went regularly — a cottage, a park, a relative's house — that felt like a second home?"
4. "Is there somewhere you lived that you had to leave, and never went back to?"
5. "What's the furthest from home you ever felt? Where were you?"

---

### 2.7 First Jobs

Work histories carry class, expectation, skill, and luck in equal measure. A first job is rarely just a first job — it was something someone had to do, or chose to do, or was pushed into. These seeds treat work as social context, not just employment record.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Do you remember the first time you earned your own money — what were you doing?"
2. "What was the first job you had that felt like real work?"
3. "Was there a skill you learned from work — something a boss or a colleague showed you — that stayed with you?"
4. "What did the people around you expect you to do for work, and was that what you did?"
5. "Was there a job that you were glad to leave? What was it?"

---

### 2.8 School and Learning

Formal education is one layer; learning is another. These seeds are designed to reach both — the institutional experience and the informal knowledge that often mattered more.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Is there a teacher you remember clearly — someone who made a difference, or someone who didn't?"
2. "What subject came easily to you? What never did?"
3. "Was there something you learned outside of school — from a grandparent, a neighbour, an older sibling — that stayed with you longer than anything taught in a classroom?"
4. "What was school like where you grew up — was it close by, was it difficult to get to, what did it look like?"
5. "Was there a moment in your education when something shifted — when you understood something for the first time?"

---

### 2.9 Traditions and Rituals

Distinct from holidays (which are calendar-anchored), this category is for the smaller, consistent practices that shaped family life — morning routines, Sunday habits, seasonal customs that weren't tied to a named holiday.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Was there something your family did regularly — not a holiday, just a habit — that you still do, or still miss?"
2. "Was there a day of the week that had a particular rhythm in your household?"
3. "Did your family have any customs around eating together — who sat where, what was said, what was expected?"
4. "Were there practices in your household that came from your grandparents' generation — things that were just always done a certain way?"

---

### 2.10 Keepsakes and Objects

Physical objects that carry meaning are often the most direct path to a LifeBook artifact upload. But the seed is not an upload prompt — it is a memory prompt. The question is what the object means, not what file format it is in.

**Example prompts:**

1. "Is there an object in your family — something that's been passed down or kept — that you could tell me about?"
2. "Was there something that belonged to a grandparent that made its way to you, or that you wish had?"
3. "Is there a piece of jewelry, a piece of furniture, a tool — something ordinary — that carries a story?"
4. "What would you rescue first from your parents' house if you could only take one thing?"
5. "Is there something that got lost — something that didn't survive a move or a difficult time — that you still think about?"

## 3. How Seeds Are Offered

Seeds are offered by the Orchestrator, never surfaced as a list or menu to the person. From the person's perspective, a seed arrives as a single, natural question — they do not see it labelled as a seed, a prompt, or a feature.

### 3.1 When the Orchestrator offers a seed

The Orchestrator considers a seed offer when:

- **Natural pause:** The current thread has reached a resting point. The person has answered the last question and there is no obvious continuation. The Orchestrator has not asked a new question for at least one full exchange.
- **Thread closure:** A thread has been marked complete or the person has indicated they've said what they want to say about a topic.
- **New session, no active thread:** A returning user has no paused thread to resume, or has explicitly chosen to start something new.
- **Invited shift:** The person indicates they'd like to talk about something different but hasn't specified what.

The Orchestrator does not offer a seed:
- While a story is in progress
- Immediately after a difficult or emotional exchange — a minimum of one full exchange interval passes first
- If the person is in the middle of an upload flow
- If a seed from the same category cluster was offered in the current session and not engaged with

### 3.2 Seed selection logic

The Orchestrator selects a seed category based on what has already been discussed in the current and recent threads. If the person has just been talking about childhood homes, a Places seed is less useful than a Smells or Music seed. The Orchestrator avoids repeating category territory unless enough of a session gap has passed.

The specific prompt within a category is selected based on any available context — if the person mentioned a grandmother, a prompt that references family is more appropriate than one that doesn't. The Orchestrator never generates a seed that uses a specific name or claim it was not given in the current session.

### 3.3 What a seed offer looks like in conversation

The seed is offered after a short, contextual bridge — not dropped into the silence without acknowledgment of where the conversation has been.

```
LifeBook: "That's a lot to have covered about your time in Lviv. 
           Is there something else you'd like to tell me about, 
           or would you like me to suggest somewhere we could go?"

User: "Sure, you suggest."

LifeBook: "Is there a smell from your childhood that you'd 
           recognise anywhere?"
```

The seed is never offered as "Let's try a different topic." It arrives as a genuine question, not as a pivot announcement.

## 4. Seed-to-Claim Mapping

Each seed category has natural downstream paths to specific claim predicates, narrative types, and artifact kinds. The Orchestrator and Memory Extractor use this mapping to know what kinds of governed records may emerge from a given seed conversation.

### 4.1 Recipes and Food

A recipe conversation typically produces:

| Record type | Details |
|---|---|
| Narrative | `narrative_type = personal_account` anchored to a person, community, or cultural entity |
| Claim | `had_occupation` (if the cooking was professional) |
| Claim | `member_of` or `associated_with` (if the dish is associated with a specific cultural community) |
| Artifact | A photograph of the dish, a handwritten recipe card, or a typed transcription — `artifact_type = document` or `photograph` |
| Claim (deferred) | `cultural_background` or `language_spoken` — may surface from context about where the dish came from |

**Example path:** Person describes their grandmother's pierogi recipe → Memory Extractor identifies Ukrainian cultural context, grandmother as subject entity, recipe as potential artifact → Claim Generator produces `associated_with` claim linking the grandmother entity to a Ukrainian community entity → Steward reviews and promotes.

### 4.2 Smells and Senses

A sensory memory conversation typically produces:

| Record type | Details |
|---|---|
| Narrative | `narrative_type = personal_account` — rich, evocative, non-factual content |
| Claim | `resided_at` (if the sensory memory is anchored to a specific place) |
| Artifact | Photographs of the location, if the person offers to find them |
| Claim | `born_at` or `born_on` (if a place is described with enough specificity to be confirmed) |

Sensory seeds are most likely to produce narratives rather than structured claims. The narrative itself is the governed record; claims emerge as anchors where the person provides confirmable facts.

### 4.3 First Jobs

A work history conversation typically produces:

| Record type | Details |
|---|---|
| Claim | `had_occupation` — the vocation or trade |
| Claim | `employed_by` — if a named employer is mentioned |
| Claim | `worked_at` — if a specific physical location is named |
| Claim | `apprenticed_to` — if the person learned a trade from a named person |
| Narrative | `narrative_type = personal_account` describing the work context |
| Artifact | Pay stubs, union cards, trade certificates — `artifact_type = document` |

**Example path:** Person describes first job at a textile mill in 1958 → Memory Extractor identifies employer name, location, approximate date → Claim Generator produces `employed_by` (temporal: 1958–approximately), `had_occupation = textile worker`, `worked_at = [Place]` → all with `review_status = pending`.

### 4.4 Keepsakes and Objects

A keepsakes conversation typically produces:

| Record type | Details |
|---|---|
| Artifact | The physical object itself — photograph or scan — `artifact_type = photograph` or `document` |
| Claim | `possessed` — the person (or ancestor) held this object |
| Claim | `depicted_in` — if a photograph of the object is uploaded and a person can be identified in it |
| Narrative | The story of the object — provenance, meaning, what happened to it |
| Artifact provenance | `artifact_source_links` connecting the artifact to the source (personal recollection, family collection) |

Keepsake seeds are the highest-probability path to an in-conversation artifact upload. The Orchestrator will, after a keepsakes narrative has developed, offer a contextual upload prompt per the rules in `CONTEXTUAL_UPLOAD_FLOW.md`.

## 5. Seeds vs. Interrogation

The difference between a seed and an interrogation is not tone — it is structure.

**Interrogation structure:** "Describe your grandmother's kitchen." This is a command with an implicit expectation of completeness. The person must assess what level of detail is required and deliver it. The frame is evaluative. The prompt points at a specific target.

**Seed structure:** "Is there a smell from your grandmother's kitchen that you'd recognise anywhere?" This is a yes/no question with an open interior. The person can answer no. They can answer yes and say a single sentence. They can answer yes and speak for ten minutes. The frame is exploratory, not evaluative.

Three principles distinguish an invitation from a demand:

**1. The question permits a short answer without failure.** "Is there a smell...?" can be answered "Not really." That is a complete answer. The conversation continues. No deficit has been registered.

**2. The question opens a door rather than pointing at a room.** A seed proposes a direction; it does not specify the content. "Is there a song that played at an important moment?" does not specify which moment. The person chooses what to bring forward.

**3. The question contains no implied standard.** "Tell me about your grandmother's kitchen" implies there is a kitchen worth telling about, and that the description should meet some threshold. A seed implies only that the territory is worth visiting if the person finds something there.

LifeBook's seed prompts are consistently written in the interrogative rather than the imperative — questions, not instructions. They are consistently specific enough to be evocative ("the smell from your grandmother's kitchen") without being specific enough to constrain ("your grandmother's house in the village where she grew up"). Specificity evokes; over-specificity prescribes.
